## Project Overview

This project focuses on understanding team quality and operational performance using Microsoft Excel. The analysis looks at how many tasks were completed, how many samples were checked, what errors were identified, and how quality patterns varied across auditors, activities, locations, and managers.

The main objective was to turn raw operational data into meaningful information that can help a team understand its quality performance and identify areas that may need further investigation.

## Business Problem

In a quality-driven operation, completing a high number of tasks is not enough. It is also important to understand the accuracy of the work and the types of errors found during quality checks.

The key questions explored in this project are:

* How much work was completed during the analysis period?
* What proportion of total tasks was checked through sampling?
* How many errors were identified in the checked samples?
* Which error categories require closer attention?
* How can quality performance be compared across auditors, activities, and locations?
* What actions could help improve the quality review process?

## Dataset Summary

The dataset covers January to December 2025 and contains 600 records across 10 columns.

The main fields include Date, Employee ID, Auditor Name, Total Tasks, Sample Checked, Errors, Error Type, Activity, Location, and Manager Name.

## Key Performance Indicators

| KPI               |  Result |
| ----------------- | ------: |
| Total Tasks       | 106,317 |
| Samples Checked   |  12,138 |
| Recorded Errors   |   2,905 |
| Sample Coverage   |  11.42% |
| Sample Error Rate |  23.93% |

Sample Coverage = Samples Checked / Total Tasks

Sample Error Rate = Recorded Errors / Samples Checked

These indicators provide an overall view of operational volume and the errors found during sample-based quality checks.

## Analysis Approach

1. Reviewed the available data fields and reporting structure.
2. Examined task volume, sample counts, and recorded errors.
3. Summarized quality information by error type and operational dimensions.
4. Compared performance across relevant groups where sample sizes allow a fair comparison.
5. Identified questions that could support further quality improvement.

## Business Insights

The dataset records 106,317 tasks, of which 12,138 were checked through sampling. This represents approximately 11.42% sample coverage.

A total of 2,905 errors were recorded in the checked samples. The resulting sample error rate is approximately 23.93%.

These results highlight the importance of monitoring both sample coverage and the errors identified during quality checks. A more detailed breakdown by error severity, activity, auditor, and location can help identify where additional review may be useful.

The overall figures alone do not establish the cause of errors. Group-level comparisons and additional operational context are needed before drawing conclusions about individual or team performance.

## Recommendations

* Review error categories to understand which types contribute most to the overall error count.
* Compare error rates alongside sample sizes instead of relying only on raw error counts.
* Investigate recurring errors by activity to identify possible training or process gaps.
* Monitor sample coverage regularly to understand how consistently work is being checked.
* Review trends over time to determine whether quality performance is improving or declining.
* Use findings to guide further investigation rather than treating the dashboard as proof of individual performance.

## Limitations

The analysis is based on the available dataset and its recorded fields. Sample-based results may not represent every task completed. Differences in sample size can also affect comparisons between auditors, activities, and locations.

The findings describe patterns in the available data and should not be treated as proof of the causes of errors.

## Conclusion

This project demonstrates how Excel can be used to organize operational data, calculate quality indicators, and support data-driven decision-making.

It also shows why a useful quality report should go beyond counting tasks and errors. Sample coverage, error rates, group-level comparisons, and clear recommendations are all important when evaluating operational quality.
